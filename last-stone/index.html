The Last Stone

A game that has been beating people since 1940. Lose first. Then work out why you didn't have to.

What this is: one investigation, start to finish, as a single interactive web page: start with one pile of stones, then move to three rows, build a bot from your own rules and watch it solve every case, find the pattern yourself, then prove it. Built with: plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, no libraries. The computer's only job is to follow the rules you give it through every position, working backward from the end, so you have data to look at. Spotting the pattern and proving it is yours.

· big type, one scene at a time, keyboard or clicker

Scene 0

Basic Nim: one pile

Start with the simplest version. One pile. On your turn, take 1, 2 or 3 stones. Whoever takes the last stone wins.

Play from 16, and you go first. Try a few games. Can you win? If you can't, is it you, or is it the position?

Now work out what's going on.

Mark every pile size where the player about to move is doomed, however well they play. Start from the end: with 0 stones left it's your move, but the other player just took the last stone, so you've already lost. Then work upward. A size is doomed if every move you have hands your opponent a size that isn't.

The doomed sizes are the multiples of 4: 0, 4, 8, 12, 16… Hand your opponent one of them and you can't lose.

Notice what you just did. You didn't guess. You started at the end and worked backward. That's called backward induction, and it is the method for the rest of this talk.

Why it can't fail

Whatever they take, you take the rest of a four. Try to break it.

Your turn

You go first from 15. Fifteen isn't a multiple of 4. What's your first move?

Basic Nim: solved. Now the advanced version: three rows, and a twist at the end.

Scene 1

Advanced Nim: the hustle

Now add rows. Three rows of stones: 3, 5 and 7. On your turn, take as many as you like from one row. And one more change: whoever takes the last stone loses.

One of you plays the bot up front. Everyone else is on their team: shout advice.

A perfect player is not unbeatable. It wins only when the position lets it. From 3 · 5 · 7 you move first, so a perfect you would win. The bot beats you here because you slip, and you'll find out exactly where.

In 1940 a machine called the Nimatron played this kind of game at the New York World's Fair. It played about 100,000 games against the public and won about 90% of them, and most of the games it lost were reportedly lost on purpose by the staff, so people would keep lining up. People have been hustling crowds with this game for 85 years. Today you work out how.

Scene 2

Build the bot

Can we work backward on three rows? Each row can be anywhere from empty to full: 4 × 6 × 8 = 192 positions. That's far too many to check by hand. But you already know the method: you used it on one pile in scene 0.

So let's write the method down as rules and hand it to a computer. Fill in every blank together, then press Launch and watch the bot do the tedious part: every position, every move. It follows our rules exactly, even if they're wrong.

Play the bot you built

It knows nothing about strategy. All it has is the table it just filled in and rule 5. It goes first from 3 · 5 · 7. If our rules are right, it can't lose from here. If we got something wrong, we can beat it.

Beat it? Press Check our rules above to find the broken one, then Change the rules and launch again. Rebuild until it can't be beaten.

The bot can tell us which positions are doomed. It can't tell us why. That part is our job.

Write a conjecture. Finish this sentence in your own words: "A position is doomed when…". Then test it on positions you haven't looked at, and hunt for one that breaks it.

Stuck?

Look at 0 · 3 · 3 and 0 · 5 · 5. Then 1 · 2 · 3, 1 · 4 · 5 and 2 · 4 · 6. In each one, the biggest row seems to be built from the other two. Built how?

Scene 3

The crack

Here are the same 24 doomed positions. Write each row in binary, where every digit stands for a power of 2: 4, 2 and 1. Then count the 1s in each column.

Before anything is explained, make a prediction: what do the columns of a doomed position have in common? Write it down. The answer below is a test of your guess, not a replacement for it.

Balanced: every binary column has an even number of 1s. Twenty of the twenty-four doomed positions are balanced.

Why it works

  1. From a balanced position, every move breaks the balance. A move changes one row, which flips at least one of its digits, so that column turns odd.
  2. From an unbalanced position, you can always restore it. Find the leftmost odd column and pick a row with a 1 there. Flip that row's digits in every odd column. Its leftmost flipped digit goes from 1 to 0, so the row gets smaller and the move is legal.

So whoever hands over a balanced position keeps control, move after move. That's the whole proof. Charles Bouton published it in 1901.

Try it

Build any position. If it's unbalanced, the button finds the move that restores the balance.

The twist

The other four doomed positions are 0 · 0 · 1, 0 · 1 · 0, 1 · 0 · 0 and 1 · 1 · 1: nothing but single stones, an odd number of them. And four balanced positions never made the list: 0 · 1 · 1, 1 · 0 · 1, 1 · 1 · 0 and the empty board. Because the last stone loses, the very end flips.

Play exactly as if the last stone wins, until only one row has more than one stone. Then leave an odd number of single stones. Try these endings. You go first.

That's the complete code:

  1. 1Two or more rows have more than one stone: make every binary column even.
  2. 2One big row left: leave an odd number of single stones.
  3. 3Only single stones left: leave an odd number.

Scene 4

Revenge

Rematch. Three rows: 3, 5, 7. The last stone loses. You go first, and this time you know the code. The binary view and the hint are there if you need them. Try not to.

You're in

You just beat the machine that beat you. Three things before you go:

  1. Your opening move from 3 · 5 · 7 is always the same: take 1 stone from any row.
  2. Don't explain it right away. Hand someone the game, let them lose, and let them ask. Then teach them how. That second part is the job.
  3. If you ever lose from a winning position, you slipped. Check the columns, then the twist.

Now flip it

A perfect player can't win from every position, and the code cuts both ways. Here the bot moves first from 1 · 2 · 3, a balanced position. Whatever it does, you can win. Play the code and see.

So the strategy is not "beat the computer". It is "know which positions are won for whoever moves, and which are lost".

Optional extension

Break the rules

One question to push on, if you want more: does the pattern from scene 0 survive when the allowed moves change? Here you may take 1 or 4 stones. In scene 0, with "take 1, 2 or 3", the doomed sizes were the multiples of 4.

Predict first. Tap every row size you think is doomed for the player about to move, then press Solve and watch the computer work its way up from 0, the same way you did with one pile. Change the rule only after you can explain this one.

The computer can find a pattern in any rule you give it. Only a proof can promise it lasts, and the proof from scene 3 is the model for it.

Scene 6

Why this belongs in a classroom

You just went through the whole cycle a math class is supposed to have: lose, wonder, investigate, prove, extend. Nobody had to be told why it mattered. You wanted revenge.

The math in it

  • Patterns and remainders: multiples of 4, then of k + 1.
  • Place value in another base, and parity: binary columns, even and odd.
  • Working backward from the end: backward induction, the idea behind recursion.
  • A real proof: two facts, and the game is solved.
  • Conjecture from data, and the difference between evidence and proof.

What the computer did

  • Opponent: it played perfectly, so it beat you only when you slipped, and lost when you played the code.
  • The bot you built: it followed your five rules through all 192 positions and 951 moves, the tedious part, so you could look for the pattern.
  • Lab (optional): it solved a changed rule in seconds, but couldn't say why the pattern holds. That part stays human.

The dare: go hustle someone this week. Then teach them how.

Optional

Why it always works

A pattern is not a proof. A game tree is: it lists every possible game from a starting pile.

Press Step and the tree labels positions from the bottom up, using one rule: a position is winning if at least one move leads to a losing position, and losing otherwise. (Flip Last stone to loses and watch 0 stones turn into a win.)

With the last stone losing, 0 stones is a win for the player to move (the other player just took the last stone), and 1 stone is a loss, because you are forced to take it.

player to move wins player to move loses columns: 1st/2nd player picks
With the last stone winning: why is 4 a square but 5 a circle?

From 4 you can leave 3, 2 or 1 stones, and all of those are circles: whoever moves next can take everything. Every move you have helps your opponent, so 4 is losing. From 5 you can take 1 and leave 4, a square, so 5 is winning.

What do the thick green lines mean?

With Highlight winning moves on, a thick line is a winning move: one that leads from a circle to a square. Every circle has at least one and no square has any. That is all a strategy is: always take a thick line.
